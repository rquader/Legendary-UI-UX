room one · a manifesto in six small parts

on weight, on memory, on resonance

field notes from a quieter web

Four rooms. This first one is an essay you scroll. Each section demonstrates the primitive it is about — drag a letter in the title above; watch the trail your cursor is leaving; look to the left of the page. Nothing here is loud. Press 2, 3, 4 to visit the others.

i.

on weight

letters that remember they are objects.

There is a strange thing about web type: it is the least material material we have. Print type was hot metal, cold metal, photo-emulsion, ink. Each step gave the letter slightly more body. Web type is a sprite sheet. It has no consequence.

The heading above is doing one small thing about that. Push a letter with your cursor. Drag one. Let it return. It knows its place better than the layout does — resize the window, and the letters will chase the new geometry home.

Ink, when it was ink, was wet. It remembered being lifted. It remembered being smudged. We can let our letters keep something of that. We can let them weigh, even a little.

ii.

on trace

a page can remember being touched.

A pointer passes through most pages like a hand through water. It enters; it leaves; the surface forgets it.

Look at the trail your cursor is leaving now. The colour is the colour of phosphor — the dust on the inside of a cathode-ray tube that glowed for a quarter of a second after the electron beam had passed. We lost the phosphor when we switched to flat panels. We should have kept it.

The page is not asking you to remember it. It is remembering you.

iii.

on memory

a thread, drawn down the left margin.

Look to the left of the page. The thin amber line is your scroll path through this essay, plotted as you read. Where you slowed, it brightened. Where you paused, a dot. Where you turned back, the line crosses itself.

It is a small honest portrait of how you are reading. You have a position, a velocity, and a history. The page can know all three — locally, in your tab — and forget them again when you close it.

Nothing is sent anywhere. The thread is yours, and ends when this tab does.

iv.

on resonance

silence is the default; sound is consent.

Below this paragraph is one small button. It is the only thing on the page that asks anything of you.

Pressing it begins a low chord — three detuned oscillators, sustained beneath everything else. The filter opens as you scroll faster. Brushing a word brushes a tone against it.

There is no song. Only the shape of the room while you are in it. The second tab visualises this room as a sky.

Once on, the chord stays — it follows you across rooms.

v.

on tide

a slow rhythm under the page.

The page is breathing. The inhalation is a small scale outward — a fifth of a percent, not enough to see if you are looking for it. The exhalation, a return. One breath every twenty-four seconds.

We read inside bodies that are doing this constantly. There is no reason a page should pretend it doesn't know.

You will stop noticing the tide in a minute. That is the point. It is meant to be felt, not seen.

vi.

on company

the cursor is a body, not a pointer.

You arrived here with a hand. Somewhere in the late nineties we gave you a tiny black arrow instead and called it a representative. The arrow has served us well for forty years and it has nothing more to teach us.

Look at the small amber dot following your cursor now. It is breathing. When you move, it follows with a quarter-second of lag — because something with weight is what it is. When you stop, it rests. In the other rooms it changes colour to match the room it is in.

It is not a pet. It is more like a hand you forgot you had.

·

on making

mostly an experiment in what AI could build.

Much of this site began as a test: what happens if you give an AI a single HTML file, no network, and permission to be strange? The primitives, the palette, the room structure, and most of the prose — including this paragraph — were largely machine-generated, shaped with feedback from a human who wanted to see whether the result could feel like a room rather than a demo.

That origin does not excuse sloppiness, but it explains the register. The voice is literary because the model reaches for literature. The herons and rivers are gone from the colour room; the synesthesia tab now talks about screens and midnight instead. The demonstrations still have to earn their keep.

If you are reading this vault in Obsidian, it tracks the same experiment — field notes written alongside the artefact, not a press kit.

— room two

on resonance, visualised

silence is the default; sound is consent.

The colours overhead are the room you are in: a starfield, and three drifting curtains of cyan, violet, and amber light. They breathe on their own. If you bring the drone on, they will breathe louder.

Click anywhere outside this text to throw a small note into the room — its pitch will be the height of where you clicked, and a soft ring of colour will mark the place. The room is small; it will catch what you give it.

Once the drone is on, it follows you across rooms. Click the dark behind these words to add notes; lower clicks are lower pitches.

— room three

on colour

words have weights and colours we never agree on.

Researchers ask synesthetes to name the colour of each letter. The answers disagree. That disagreement is the whole point: there is no universal chart, only private ones. The mapping below is another fiction — a palette chosen so the room could show you what averaging feels like.

Each letter has a hue I gave it. Each word's colour is the average of its letters'. Hover any word in the passage — the room will take its colour for as long as you are looking. The letters themselves will lift their grey gauze.

The screen is grey until a word asks it otherwise. Open any tab late at night and the interface pretends it has no temperature — flat, obedient, politely invisible. But language was never flat. Say subway and something underground shifts toward rust. Say lemon and a thin yellow arrives before you taste it. Say midnight and the room you are in disagrees about how dark that is. Hover here. Each letter carries a colour I assigned. Each word borrows the average of its letters. The fiction is deliberate: I wanted to see whether a page could blush when you looked at the right noun. No synesthete would confirm this chart. Good. The chart was never a claim. It was a way to make pigment legible for sixty seconds.

letters of the word you are over

The words are inertial — your cursor pushes them. The technique borrows the spirit of pretext by Cheng Lou: text geometry as arithmetic over canvas-measured widths, not the DOM's accident.

— room four

on attention

the words you linger on become stars.

This is a quiet paragraph for you to read at the speed of your patience. Hover any word for a moment and it will ignite — a small star drawn in the field behind these lines. The next word you linger on will join it, and a thin line will draw itself between the two. By the time you reach this paragraph's final period, a private constellation will have appeared, made of nothing but the places you stayed.

There are no wrong stars. There is also no save button: the constellation lives only as long as this tab does. That is on purpose. Some things are meant to be seen once, by exactly one person, and then released.

Try these, as you read them: morning, anchor, mercury, vellum, river, sleep, copper, dusk, hawthorn, blue. Or any others, anywhere on the page. The eye that lingers is the only instrument.
